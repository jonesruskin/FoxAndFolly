## Description

Every night, when no one was watching, a young sunflower turned around. She didn't know why.

Hesper was born in a field of ten thousand flowers, and from her first morning she followed the Sun: east at dawn, south at noon, west at dusk. The old flowers around her never moved. They stood heavy and still, faces fixed on the east, and Hesper was sure they had given up. She promised herself she never would… until the morning she bloomed, and her body wouldn't turn anymore.

*Facing East* is an original animated fable about ambition, ageing, and the difference between stillness and surrender.

🌻 **The real science:** young sunflowers really do track the sun across the sky and swing back to face east every night, driven by an internal clock. When they mature, they stop and face east for good. In a 2016 study published in *Science* (Atamian et al.), east-facing heads warmed faster in the morning and drew about five times more pollinators than west-facing ones.

## Pinned comment

🌻 A few things hidden in *Facing East*:

1. **Her name.** *Hesper* comes from Hesperus, the evening star. In Greek the word also means "western". A flower named for the west who learns to face east.
2. **The opening line is literally true.** Young sunflowers really do turn back to face east during the night, before sunrise. It's an internal circadian clock, not the sun, that tells them to. Hesper's body was learning to face the dawn from the very first scene.
3. **Every sunflower face in the film uses real sunflower maths.** Each floret is placed at 137.5° (the golden angle) from the last, which creates the Fibonacci spirals you can see turning in the final close-up.
4. **Watch the old ones in the afternoon scene.** Their faces are in shadow with the sun on their backs. At dawn, the same flowers are the first to light up.
5. **The music has two themes:** a rising four notes for Hesper chasing the Sun and a falling version for the nights she turned back. Listen for the rising theme returning, finally complete, at sunrise.

A science footnote: the "five times more pollinators" figure comes from a 2016 *Science* study. A 2024 study in commercial plantations didn't find the same effect, so the jury's still out on how much it matters in big farm fields. Nature is rarely as tidy as a fable.
