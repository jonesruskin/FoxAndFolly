## Description

The end of the world arrived on an ordinary day, in the middle of love.

Hell Creek, 66 million years ago. Old Horn is an elderly Triceratops, nearly blind, with a broken horn and a notched frill. Wisp is a young, feathered Pectinodon who sleeps in the curve of her frill and has become her eyes. Together they share one last ordinary day: a morning by the lake, a white pebble found in the shallows, a hunter in the ferns, and a night spent counting stars beneath a strange new one that grows brighter every night.

At dawn, a second sun rises in the south. For the first time in years, Old Horn sees light.

*SECOND SUN* is an original animated short about companionship, courage and the fragile ordinariness of every day we're given.

🦖 **The real science:** the film's final act follows the sequence recorded at the Tanis fossil site in North Dakota, about 3,000 km from the Chicxulub impact crater. After the asteroid struck, the ground shook, the water surged, and tiny beads of glass (impact spherules, rock melted in the impact) rained from the sky within minutes to hours (DePalma et al., *PNAS*, 2019). The new "star" in the nights before the impact is artistic licence.
The animals are drawn from real Hell Creek life:
- *Triceratops*, with its solid bony frill.
- *Pectinodon*, a small feathered troodontid with huge eyes.
- *Edmontosaurus*, a duck-billed dinosaur with a fleshy head comb.
- A young *Tyrannosaurus*.
- A tiny early mammal like *Purgatorius*. Mammals like it survived.

🦖 A few things hidden in *SECOND SUN*:

1. **The same camera, three times.** The dawn lake at the start, the dusk lake before the star-counting, and the grey lake after the ash are filmed from the exact same position. Pause them side by side.
2. **The Saddle.** Look for the notch in the far ridgeline. It appears in the first morning, at the second sunrise, in the ash, through 66 million years of deep time, and on the horizon of the badlands where they're found. The land changed; the Saddle didn't.
3. **The star's warning.** The new star is visible long before anyone notices it: as a faint dot in the daytime sky while the hatchlings climb out of their nest (around 1:15), and brighter every time we see it after that.
4. **Its sound, too.** The star has its own musical note, a glassy G sharp. It's a tritone against the film's home key of D, the most unsettled interval in music. You first hear it in the opening seconds.
5. **The last sound in the film** is one high note answered by one low note: his chirp and her hum, the same call and answer they used to count stars.
6. **The survivor.** The small mammal Wisp chases all morning is the one creature that climbs out of the ash. Our own ancestors were small burrowing mammals like it.
7. **Found together.** The fossil in the final scenes still has her broken brow horn, the notch in her frill, and the white pebble between his tiny claws.

A science footnote: Tanis records the impact's aftermath within minutes to hours. The "star" in the nights before is our invention; the rest of the final act is drawn from what the rocks preserve.
